# GP holes stay new; the symmetry rule does not

As of November 2026 no symbolic regression method leads on every benchmark, and no published method was found that does what the GP-holes prototype does: put a Gaussian-process prior on the unknown part of an expression, integrate it out exactly, and score every partial expression of a search by that evidence (the research notes put the chance that no closer work exists at about 75 to 80%). Two of the four points that `NOTES.md` lists as new do not hold as stated: the rule "symmetry" is published for neural surrogates (section 6 of NestyNet III, arXiv, August 2026, solves nearly the same linear condition on the gradient and derives the same forms), and an answer that is part formula and part unknown function has precedents in SHAREs, in "formula plus GP remainder" models and in one example of NestyNet III. On the SRBench Feynman problems the best independently measured recovery rate is 53% without noise, on up to 10,000 points (self-reported rates run from 58.5% to a claimed 100% with units given), and 10% noise halves these or worse; PySR is the most consistent search-based baseline across independent studies. No benchmark scores symbolic recovery automatically at about 100 noisy points and no surrogate-based method reports a result there, so that regime is the prototype's strongest claim and also something it has to measure itself. The present comparison (PySR for 60 s, three seeds, success judged by the error outside the training box) gives the baseline 30 to 500 times less time than published comparisons do and rests on a criterion that the literature uses only as a supporting number. The techniques to adopt first are a closed-form test for adding a linear term beside a GP (Zhu et al. 2022), which screens the library without a fit per candidate, and costs for constants and a rounding rule taken from description-length scores, in place of the hand-set 3 and 10. One source that bears on novelty could not be opened, the workshop paper of Cohrs et al.; its abstract and the authors' code point away from a GP or a marginalised function. Below, a statement with a link is what a source says; a statement marked as our inference is reasoning from the research notes or from this synthesis, and none of the proposed changes has been tried in the prototype.

## No benchmark scores symbolic recovery at 100 noisy points

### Benchmarks with a known answer use thousands of clean points and hours per run

The two benchmarks that referees know best count exact recovery on large, clean samples. **SRBench 2021** has 130 problems (116 Feynman, 14 Strogatz), trains on 75% of the data capped at 10,000 rows, adds Gaussian noise to the target at 0, 0.1%, 1% and 10% of its root mean square, and gives each of 10 trials "1M evaluations or 8 hours" on one core, with each method tuned beforehand from six settings ([SRBench Table 2, App. A.4, A.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.14351)). A result is a "symbolic solution" if, after SymPy simplification, its difference from the true formula is a constant or its ratio to it is a non-zero constant; the authors add that they "cannot guarantee that all symbolic solutions are captured with perfect fidelity by this metric" ([SRBench Sec. 4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.14351)). **SRSD** resamples 120 Feynman formulas in realistic SI ranges with true constants, sorts them into 30 easy, 40 medium and 50 hard, adds a second set with one to three irrelevant inputs, and trains on 8,000 points; each method runs up to 100 sessions in a 24-hour job, and the best one on validation data is scored by R² > 0.999, by the SRBench solution rate and by a normalised tree edit distance ([SRSD Sec. 3 to 5, App. A](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10540)). The current edition, **SRBench 2025**, dropped the Feynman and Strogatz track and with it the symbolic score: it has 24 data sets, 30 runs, 6 hours of tuning plus 1 hour of fitting on one core, and ranks by test R² and model size. Its repository tells authors of new methods to "use this roster and these results rather than the 2021 tables" ([SRBench 2.0 Sec. 1, 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03977); [SRBench README](https://github.com/cavalab/srbench)).

Only two published settings sit in the prototype's range of points, and neither scores recovery automatically. EmpiricalBench was judged by eye, "as oftentimes automated equality checking with sympy produced incorrect results" ([PySR Sec. 3.2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01582)), and the first-principles track of SRBench 2025 only places each result on a plot of accuracy against size beside the accepted law ([SRBench 2.0 Sec. 3.3, 5.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03977)). Our inference: a protocol at 100 noisy points has to be assembled, most naturally from the SRSD formulas, whose sampling answers the main criticisms of the Feynman data and which come with dummy-input sets, subsampled as FastSRB did and with the noise levels of SRBench.

How success is counted moves the numbers by a large factor. Five meanings are in use: SymPy difference or ratio, a hit anywhere on the front judged by eye, a match to a curated list, a numeric test far outside the training box, and the verdict of a language model. With curated lists in place of SymPy, 200 points and 30 minutes, PySR's rate on SRSD "increases from 26.7% to 44.7%" ([FastSRB Sec. 4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14481)). Fit does not stand in for recovery: about 45% of DSR's and all of E2E's equations with R² > 0.999 on the SRSD dummy sets used a dummy input, and in a study with 23 researchers human judgement correlated with edit distance (−0.416) and not with R² (0.005) ([SRSD Tables 6, 7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10540)). Error outside the training range, which is the prototype's present criterion, is used as an extra axis by the 2022 competition, LLM-SRBench and SymbolicArena, with the competition's caveat that "a certain dataset can have many different solutions inside the interpolation region that behaves differently outside this region" ([competition Sec. V-B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01117)). The README's own remark that this criterion "also counts approximations as right" is the same point.

PySR is almost never run at its factory settings, which have the operators + − × ÷ only, 100 iterations, 31 populations of 27 and a maximum size of 30, and which return the highest-scoring member of the front whose loss is within 1.5 times that of the most accurate ([PySR source](https://github.com/MilesCranmer/PySR/blob/master/pysr/sr.py)). SRSD ran it with sin, cos, exp and log, nesting limits, 50,000 iterations and a timeout of 82,800 s; SymbolicArena with 8 populations of 64 and six unary operators for 3 hours ([SRSD Table 20](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10540); [SymbolicArena Table 17](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35113)). Left at "default budgets", PySR stopped by itself after 99.6 s per equation on Nguyen, 150.6 s on Strogatz and 619.6 s on Korns ([MOSAIC-SR Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20997)). Operon's Python wrapper defaults to a population of 1,000, 1,000 generations, 1M evaluations and a final choice by minimum description length ([pyoperon source](https://github.com/heal-research/pyoperon/blob/main/pyoperon/sklearn.py)).

The prototype's comparison, as `compare_pysr.py` and the README have it, sets the operators by hand (+ − × ÷, sin, cos, exp, log, sqrt, square), so the notes' concern about PySR's bare default operators does not apply to it. Three gaps remain. **The budget:** 60 s on 4 threads is about 4 CPU-minutes, roughly 8 times less than FastSRB's 30 single-process minutes, the shortest budget found in any published comparison. **The seeds:** 3, where the studies above that state a number use 5 to 30, except SymbolicArena with 3. **The criterion,** as above. The maintainers of SRBench propose that each method define "a small set (e.g., four) of hyperparameter configurations", each limited to about one hour, with the default always included because "some methods perform best with their default settings"; they budget by wall time and name single-core against multi-core fairness as an open issue ([SRBench 2.0 Sec. 2, 5.3, 5.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03977)).

### What a comparison that referees accept would contain

No source states what referees require; what follows is our inference from what published comparisons did. One operator set for all methods, stated. PySR and Operon at the prototype's own wall time and cores and again at about one hour, the SRBench 2025 and competition figure, so that "equal cost" and "baseline at its best" are both shown; a curve of solution rate against budget answers the objection directly. The default plus about four settings, chosen without looking at the test criterion. At least 5 and better 10 to 30 seeds per problem with a paired test. Symbolic recovery by one stated procedure applied to every method as the headline, with a manual check of near-misses, and beside it the error outside the range, an edit distance or Jaccard index, and the size. Both the equation PySR selects and the best on its front, since EmpiricalBench counts the front. Crashes and timeouts as failures.

For the roster, PySR, Operon and one of uDSR or DSO are what current studies run, and PhySO where units are given. AI Feynman is no longer in the rosters of SRBench 2025, ERBench or SymbolicArena and sat at the 8-hour limit in SRBench 2021 ([SRBench 2.0 Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03977); [SRBench results](https://github.com/cavalab/srbench/tree/master/results)). For this method a referee can still ask for a member of its own family (AI Feynman 2.0, or NestyNet-SR if its code runs at 100 points), a Bayesian method that scores complete expressions, plain GP regression, and compositional kernel search as the nearest method on the GP side. A result with a hole has no category in any existing score: the notes found no published discussion of how to score a partly specified answer, so that rule has to be defined in advance. The partial-credit measures (edit distance in SRSD, the Jaccard index over subexpressions in UDFS and ERBench) are the nearest instruments, and SHAREs reports the fit for each number of unknown functions ([UDFS Sec. 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19626); [SHAREs Sec. 5.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09788)).

Later papers report higher rates under protocols of their own: PhySO 58.5% with units, "only surpassed by uDSR" without noise; ParFam's reruns find that among the five best methods "PySR performs the best, followed by ParFam" for the symbolic rate; uDSR and UDFS give their rates only in figures ([PhySO Sec. Across the independent studies PySR is first or second for recovery wherever it was run: 35 of 45 on EmpiricalBench against 22 for DSR and 9 for Operon, 0.29 ± 0.04 on ERBench's secret set against 0.05 for gplearn and 0 for DSR, E2E and Operon, second on four axes of SymbolicArena behind iMCTS and FePySR ([PySR Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01582); [ERBench Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09276); [SymbolicArena Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35113)). Its reach is limited by formula size: recovery is "near-perfect" for 1 to 3 operators and "near zero for problems with more than 13 operators" ([ERBench Sec. 5.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09276)). The organisers of the 2022 competition conclude that "there is hardly a single winner or a single approach that could be named state-of-the-art" ([competition Sec. V-A](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01117)). For the cost of a run: in SRBench 2021 the median training time without noise was 635 s for DSR and 2,015 s for Operon, with AI Feynman at the 8-hour limit ([SRBench results](https://github.com/cavalab/srbench/tree/master/results)). The prototype's 10 to 60 s is short against all of these.

Bayesian methods score complete expressions and are slow. The Bayesian machine scientist runs a Metropolis chain over trees with 40 tempered replicas and scores by BIC plus a prior fitted to 4,080 Wikipedia formulas; exhaustive symbolic regression enumerates one-input trees to complexity 10, at 33 to 150 CPU-hours for one benchmark of 10⁵ points; the sequential Monte Carlo method of Bomarito and Leser moves a population of 2,000 expressions ([Guimerà et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12157); [ESR Sec. II, V](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11461); [SMC-SR Sec. 1.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10849)). None has an element for an unknown function, and none of the methods read in that literature abstains or returns a formula with a part that is explicitly unknown.

Pretrained transformers and language models do not change the ranking for recovery. E2E solves 0% of SRSD by the symbolic criterion, and NeSymReS and E2E recover 2.44% and 10.40% of the Feynman set averaged over four noise levels; the models accept at most 3 or 10 inputs and 200 to 500 points ([SRSD Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10540); [MDLformer Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03753); [NeSymReS Sec. 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06427); [E2E Sec. 2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10532)). In every working language-model system the model proposes structure and a numerical optimiser fits the constants; on Feynman problems LLM-SR "rapidly achieves low data-driven errors within few iterations (<20) ... suggesting a recitation of memorized information", and on a benchmark built to block recall the best system reaches 31.5% symbolic accuracy ([LLM-SR Sec. 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18400); [LLM-SRBench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10415)). In the one controlled study of frontier coding agents on an unpublished formula, 4 of 18 single-prompt runs found the complete formula, all four with a correct hint ([agents study, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00435)). None of these methods returns a partly resolved answer or says that the data do not decide.

### Ten per cent noise halves recovery even with thousands of points

The SRBench table shows three patterns (our reading). Methods that divide the problem or fit many constants lose almost everything by 1% noise: AI Feynman from 52.6 to 12.6%, Operon from 16.0 to 1.9%. Methods with few or no fitted constants are flat: DSR, gplearn. Methods between lose half. The surrogate family behaves alike under its own protocols: NestyNet-SR falls from 120 solved to 105, 94 and 56 of 120 at noise of 0.1%, 1% and 10% of σ_y; KAN-SR on the SRSD easy set from 93.3 to 53.3%; AI Feynman 2.0 reports 73 of 100 at noise 10⁻¹ ([NestyNet III Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21051); [KAN-SR Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10089); [AI Feynman 2.0 Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10782)). Strong priors on structure hold up: PhySO, with units, "continues to accurately recover expressions over 53% of the time" at 10% noise, where it quotes uDSR at 10.7% ([PhySO Sec. IV.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03192)). The only table found with PySR against noise on the Feynman set is in a language-model paper: 48.3, 34.2, 31.7 and 25.8% at noise 0, 0.1%, 1% and 10%, with Operon at 20.8, 7.5, 0 and 0% ([IdeaSearchFitter Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08317)). Noise also raises size where parsimony is weak: the median simplified complexity from no noise to 10% goes from 35 to 78 for Operon and from 12 to 13 for DSR ([SRBench results](https://github.com/cavalab/srbench/tree/master/results)).

### Below a few hundred points almost nothing is measured

One 2026 table gives recovery against sample size, on 40 Feynman equations and apparently clean data: PySR 96.5% at 5 points per input dimension rising to 100% at 80, an enumeration method 100% throughout ([ERBench Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09276)). AI Feynman found most equations by polynomial fit and enumeration "using only 10 data points", while those that needed the network "need significantly more data points (between 10² and 10⁶)" ([AI Feynman Sec. III.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11481)). Our inference from the first table: at this size the difficulty comes from noise and from formulas that cannot be told apart in the sampled range, not from the number of points alone. A curve of solution rate against N, with noise, for several methods between 20 and 500 points appears to be unpublished; the prototype could supply it, and it would be new measured content.

On small real data all current methods return expressions several times larger than the accepted law. In the first-principles track of SRBench 2025 "only ITEA successfully retrieved one of the first-principles equations (Leavitt)", and on the 30-row Newton set the median sizes are 18 to 55 nodes against a law of 7 to 9 ([SRBench 2.0 Sec. 5.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03977); [SRBench 2025 results](https://github.com/cavalab/srbench/tree/srbench_2025/results)). Evidence-type scores stop this and then err the other way. With a marginal-likelihood fitness on 20 points the number of constants stops growing where an RMSE fitness overfits; on 12 hard Feynman problems with 500 points and 10% noise the sequential Monte Carlo method overfits on 5 of 12 against almost all for genetic baselines; with 32 points and 20% errors the generating form ranks 39th under a description-length score, behind θ0·x² ([Bomarito et al. 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220001900/downloads/smcbingo_gecco22.pdf); [SMC-SR Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10849); [ESR Table I](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11461)).

That last case is a limit of the data, not of the search. Fajardo-Fontiveros et al. show "a transition from a low-noise phase in which the true model can be learned, to a phase in which the observation noise is too high for the true model to be learned by any method", moving to higher noise as N grows, with a bound from the competition with the simplest model ([Fundamental limits, abstract, Eqs. 6 to 9](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02704)). Our inference, from the same algebra applied to any simpler competitor at N = 100: a wrong formula whose RMS difference from the truth over the data is below about 0.25 noise standard deviations (for a structure cost 3 nats lower and equally many constants), or below 0.58 (10 nats and two constants fewer), is preferred by any score of this type, whatever it does outside the range. This is the published form of the weakness that `NOTES.md` lists as "a formula that fits within the noise is preferred to a hole"; the literature treats it as a property of the data and answers it by reporting the near-optimal set ([ESR Sec. V](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11461); [Guimerà & Sales-Pardo Sec. 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19540)).

## Closed-form screening and principled costs are the first things to adopt

Three of these come first, in our judgement. The closed-form test of Zhu et al. acts on the largest cost of a run at low risk, because it only orders candidates that the evidence then judges. The Fisher-information cost and the description-length snapping remove what a referee will question (the reply of the AI Feynman 2.0 authors presented the removal of "arbitrary hyperparameter-determined thresholds" as that paper's advance, [author feedback](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2020/file/33a854e247155d590883b93bca53848a-AuthorFeedback.pdf)) and give the exact constants that a symbolic score needs. The items that refine the rule "symmetry" are the least certain. NestyNet III states that its symmetry discovery is "certifying only while gradients are accurate to ∼10⁻³" and that the operator "abstains" on 945 real points with 0.12 dex scatter ([NestyNet III Sec. 11.3, 11.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21051)). At 100 noisy points such relations can propose and cannot certify, which is how the prototype uses them, so the realistic gain is fewer fits, not more recoveries.

Not worth adopting at this scale, by the papers' own numbers: thresholded Taylor coefficients for separability (correct for 3 of 24, [TaylorGP Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09751)); Meijer G-functions (1 to 4 s per evaluation, [SMPF Sec. 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04791)); gradient search over the EML operator tree (about 25% recovery at depths 3 to 4 on noise-free data, [EML Sec. 4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21852)); and language-model proposals, which need a GPU or network, about 1,000 calls per problem, and lean on recall ([SR-Scientist Sec. 4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11661)).

### NestyNet III section 6 is the symmetry rule for a neural surrogate

For the affine vector field V = (A·x + b)·∇ + (α + β·y)·∂_y the paper requires "∇f(x)·(Ax + b) − α − βf(x) = 0" and notes that it "is linear in all n² + n + 2 unknowns jointly, so every sampled point contributes one row of a design matrix, and the full Lie algebra of affine symmetries of the target is the nullspace of the stacked system, obtained by a single SVD". An accepted generator gives f = −α/β + e^{βs}·H(z) or f = α·s + H(z), with z the invariant coordinates; the same solve in the chart log x "turns scalings into translations, so monomial coordinates ∏ x_i^{a_i} appear as linear invariant covectors whose components are snapped to small rationals", and the chart 1/x is used as well ([NestyNet III Sec. 6.1, 6.2, Eqs. 8, 9](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21051)). Our inference from the README's description of the rule: the prototype's relations of one to three inputs, Σ c_j·∂H/∂s_j = γ·H, = δ or = 0 in a coordinate s, are this equation with a pure translation in each chart, solved the same way by the smallest singular value, and its two normal forms are the paper's. NestyNet's field is more general, since a full matrix A covers rotations and Lorentz boosts. Earlier and narrower versions are in DDRSR (constant ratios of two partial derivatives give c1·x1 + c2·x2 and x1^c1·x2^c2), in AI Feynman 2.0 (the direction of the gradient) and, for power laws, in Constantine et al. ([DDRSR Sec. 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02628); [AI Feynman 2.0 Sec. 2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10782); [Constantine et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04303)).

What differs is the surrogate and the decision. NestyNet uses a neural network with analytic derivatives and compares candidates by loss; the series does consider a GP and sets it aside: on 5,000 points a tuned GP and the network reach "essentially the same accuracy", and the reasons against the GP are its O(N³) cost and that it is "not designed to be composed" ([NestyNet I Sec. 8.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05862)). Neither reason binds at 100 points. Section 6's own demonstrations run "in oracle mode", with exact function values and exact gradients, and solve 88 of 115 equations; the text does not say whether the symmetry layer was active in the run that solved 120 of 120; and on real data the operator abstains ([NestyNet III Sec. 11.1, 11.2, 11.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21051)). Not found in Part III: the prototype's second-order relations for a sine factor and for a hole that is linear in u^p. The notes on Parts I, II and IV do not report them either, but those parts were not searched for this point. Two details matter for a manuscript. The paper uses "hole" for something else, a place in a near-miss expression to be refilled ([NestyNet III Sec. 5.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21051)). And its SPARC example is the nearest published case of a hole that stays: the inner coordinate is symbolic, five closed forms for the outer law fit within the bootstrap uncertainty of one another, and the authors "report the outer law as a family ... and promote no unique interpolation function", with a posterior for its local slope from a linearised Laplace approximation around one fitted network ([NestyNet III Sec. 11.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21051)).

The model class alone is not a contribution: a·H(u) is a GP with kernel a(x)·k(u(x), u(x′))·a(x′) by standard kernel algebra (the notes mark the textbook reference as not checked), and with a polynomial a and a single raw input it is already inside the grammar of kernel search. The notes' one-line placement is "compositional kernel search in which base kernels are parametrised by symbolic functions and the search prefers rank-one kernels" (our inference). The objection to expect is therefore no longer "AI Feynman with a GP" but, in the notes' words, "NestyNet-SR / AI Feynman with a GP surrogate, scored like the Automatic Statistician", and the answer rests on the first two rows and the last.

One weakness is shared with the nearest works and will be raised. The evidence does not by itself single out the symbolic explanation when the GP can also fit: Zhu et al. steer to it by initialisation, SJEPA states that "the symbolic–GP decomposition is not identifiable from predictive fit alone", and kernel search needs BIC ([Zhu et al. Sec. 3, 4](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/181580/); [SJEPA Sec. 6.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.04060)). The prototype's costs play that role, and their values are a modelling choice of the same kind. Unlike NEAR's loss, the evidence of a hole is also not a bound on the scores of its refinements, so the greedy search carries no guarantee.

### What reviewers asked of the nearest papers

Public reviews exist for two of them. Reviewers of AI Feynman 2.0 called the system "full of heuristics and relatively slow", wanted "to see some failure cases", asked "Do the authors expect the system to function reasonably on small training sets (say, tens of points)?" and whether a network that overfits the noise misleads the method, and called the improvements "somewhat incremental" ([NeurIPS 2020 reviews](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/file/33a854e247155d590883b93bca53848a-Review.html)). Of NEAR they asked for a synthetic test with a known answer, an empirical check that the heuristic is admissible, a comparison with "a small neural network ... that predicts the residual after running your interpretable model", and one called it "yet another heuristic for performing a search over program space" ([NeurIPS 2020 reviews](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2020/file/342285bb2a8cadef22f667eeb6a63732-Review.html)). Our inference for this method: beside the comparison described above, a reviewer will want the rate at which the evidence prefers a wrong refinement to the hole it came from, at each noise level, the search with each rule and each cost left out in turn, failure cases, and one real or closed-form-free target where the partial answer is the point.

## One unread paper and a dozen conflicting numbers limit these conclusions

### What could not be read or checked

**Cohrs et al.** (ICLR 2024 workshop, "Semiparametric inference and equation discovery with the Bayesian machine scientist") was not opened: OpenReview answered every request with a verification page, and no other copy was found. The account rests on three things. The abstract proposes a "hybrid Bayesian machine scientist" for the Q10 model of ecosystem respiration and says it "extracts the correct equations" ([ICLR page](https://www.iclr.cc/virtual/2024/21401)). The first author's later repository describes hybrid forms fp + fa, fp·fa and fp/fa in which the learned part is "neural networks or symbolic regression", lists the symbolic variant as "Coming Soon", and contains no file with "Gaussian process", "marginal" or "kernel" ([repository](https://github.com/KaiHCohrs/semi-param-hybrid-modeling)). Four of the five authors describe the machine scientist's score in November 2026 as BIC plus a structural prior ([arXiv 2611.12458](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12458)). Our inference: the unknown function is searched symbolically from the start, and "semiparametric" names the problem of a parameter of interest beside an unknown function. The overlap is the problem class, a known part plus or times an unknown part; the treatment is the opposite of a hole. Two things to check in the full text at the [OpenReview link](https://openreview.net/forum?id=7YqV4deFAc): that the unknown part is a machine-scientist expression throughout, with no network or GP fitted inside the search, and that the score is the unmodified description length. Points, noise levels and results of that paper are not known.

This report was written from the research notes; no source was reopened for it, so a misreading in a note carries through. The other gaps, by what they affect. For novelty: OpenReview could not be searched for submissions under review, Google Scholar was not queried, and citation lists came from OpenAlex, which searches titles and abstracts only, so a paper that mentions a GP prior on a subexpression only in its body would be missed. The statistics literature on semiparametric model selection was not searched beyond two Bayes-factor papers, nor were latent force models and GPs constrained by linear operators. The Neurocomputing version of Otte and Liu et al. 2021 were read through abstracts or summaries. NestyNet Parts I, II and IV were read in the sections cited, not line by line, and the "gradient-noise ladder" behind the 10⁻³ figure is only in the code repository. ## Conclusion

The claim that survives the literature is narrower and firmer than the one in `NOTES.md`. It is not a principle for the rules, which NestyNet III published first, and not the partial answer as such, which SHAREs argues for on scientific grounds. It is that the evidence is one currency for every state of the search, from plain GP regression to a finished formula, so that a surrogate, a partial expression and a formula are compared as models of the same data, and that this works where no method with a neural surrogate has shown a result: about 100 noisy points. NestyNet's authors measured, on one smooth three-input case with 5,000 points, that a GP matches their surrogate, and rejected it for costs that do not bind at this size; that leaves the small-data variant open and gives a citable reason for it. On this reading the method is new enough for a paper, on two conditions: the full text of Cohrs et al. confirms what its abstract and code suggest, and the data regime is shown and not asserted.

The second condition turns the evaluation into part of the contribution. The curve of recovery against the number of points under noise, for this method beside PySR and Operon at equal cost and at one hour, does not exist in the literature, and neither does a rule for scoring an answer with a hole. The harder question a referee can ask is not about priority but about the costs of 3 and 10 that decide between a hole and a formula; the published answers are a cost per constant that follows from the Fisher information and a per-problem statement of whether the true formula was the best-scoring model at all, which the prototype's `truth.py` already computes.
