## 1. For each paper: the algorithm, the structure tests, how each test is computed and thresholded

### Takeaway
Every method makes the same three moves: fit a smooth surrogate, test the surrogate for structure that removes inputs (merge a pair, split additively or multiplicatively, find an inner coordinate) and recurse, then close the small pieces by polynomial fit, enumeration or one-dimensional library matching. They differ in what the tests read (function values in AIF1, gradient directions in AIF2, second derivatives in KAN2 and NestyNet-SR, Taylor coefficients in TaylorGP), and the thresholds are multiples of the surrogate's validation error or hand-set tolerances; no paper uses a likelihood to decide a test.

### Cited Findings

#### AI Feynman (AIF1)
- Overall: a sequence of modules tried in turn (dimensional analysis, polynomial fit, brute force, then neural-network tests); when a test succeeds the transformed problem, with fewer variables, is sent to a fresh run of the whole algorithm (Sec. II.1, Fig. 1 and 2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Dimensional analysis: units of each variable are a vector of 5 integers; with M the matrix of input units and b the output units, solve Mp = b and take a null-space basis U (MU = 0); new dimensionless inputs are products of powers given by U, output is y divided by the product given by p. The freedom in p and U is used to make as many entries zero as possible, which "typically results in the resulting powers of the dimensionless variables being integers" (Sec. II.2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Polynomial fit: degrees 0 to 4 by linear least squares; success if r.m.s. error is at most 10⁻⁴ (Sec. II.3, Table 2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Brute force: all syntactically valid reverse-Polish strings in order of length, over three symbol subsets tried in turn; two variants also solve for one additive or one multiplicative constant; stops when the maximum error is below a threshold or at a time limit (Sec. II.4, Table 1) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Surrogate: fully connected net, 6 hidden layers (3×128, 3×64) with softplus, trained on 100,000 points (80% train), 100 epochs, Adam, weight decay 10⁻²; validation errors obtained were between 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁵ of the r.m.s. of f; the net is used only if its validation error is below 10⁻² (Sec. II.5.1, Table 2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Pair symmetry test (Algorithm 1): for each pair (i, j), shift both inputs by a = 1 and compute error = RMSE(net(x), net(x_shifted)) / RMSE(net(x)); if error < 7 × (validation error), replace the pair by its difference. The same is done for sum, product and ratio (ratio = scaling symmetry). The factor 7 was chosen "after experimentation ... which roughly optimized the training set performance" (Sec. II.5.2, Alg. 1) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Separability test (Eq. 3, Algorithm 2): with c the vector of input means, multiplicative: Δ = |f(x₁,x₂) − f(x₁,c₂) f(c₁,x₂)/f(c₁,c₂)| / f_rms; additive: RMSE(net(x), net(x₁-part) + net(x₂-part) − net(c)) / RMSE(net(x)). Separable if the r.m.s. over the data is below 10 × (validation error). All subsets of variables are tried. A variant tests f = a + g(x₁)h(x₂) (footnote 2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- After a split, the sub-problems are data sets generated from the surrogate: y′ = f(x₁, c₂) and y″ = f(c₁, x₂)/f(c₁, c₂); once y′ is solved, y″ is redefined from the real data as y/y′ times the numerical constant (Sec. II.5.3) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Setting two variables equal: solve the reduced problem on the surrogate, then try to solve y divided by that solution (Sec. II.5.4) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Extra transformations: polynomial fit and brute force are rerun on y transformed by sqrt, square, log, exp, inverse, sin, cos, tan, arcsin, arccos, arctan; the same transforms (plus multiply and divide by 2) are applied to input variables one at a time (Sec. II.6) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- All thresholds in one place (Table 2): brute force 10⁻⁵, polynomial 10⁻⁴, net usable below 10⁻², separability 10 ε_NN, symmetry 7 ε_NN, brute force and polynomial after a split 10 ε_NN, λ = N_d^(1/2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)

#### AI Feynman 2.0 (AIF2)
- Stated motivation: AIF1 found structure "in an unprincipled and ad hoc way"; AIF2 adds gradient-based tests for general modularity, replaces fixed thresholds by a Pareto frontier of description length against inaccuracy, replaces max-error rejection by a hypothesis test, and adds normalizing flows for densities (Sec. 1) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Strategy list (Table 1): negativity (solve −f), positivity (solve ln f), additive and multiplicative separability (as in AIF1), simple symmetry (pair replaced by +, −, ×, /), compositionality f = g(h(x)), generalized symmetry f = g[h(x′), x″], generalized additivity f(x₁,x₂) = F[g(x₁)+h(x₂)], and zero, integer and rational snap plus re-optimisation of parameters — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Compositionality: by the chain rule the unit gradient of f equals ± the unit gradient of h (Eq. 1), so h is searched for by brute force with per-point loss L_d[1 − |ŷ·∇̂f_k(x_i)|], where ŷ is the surrogate's unit gradient (Sec. 2.1, 2.2) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Generalized symmetry: if k inputs x′ enter only through h(x′), the unit gradient with respect to x′ does not depend on the other inputs x″ (Eq. 2); this is tested "without knowing h". Test (Appendix A): at a data point, compute unit gradients v̂_j for the same x′ and m other data points' x″; V = 1 − max over unit μ of (1/m) Σ (μ·v̂_j)², i.e. one minus an eigenvalue of the matrix (1/m) Σ v̂_j v̂_jᵀ; V is 0 when all v̂_j agree. V is computed for every subset of up to n_g = 3 variables and the subset with the smallest median V is taken — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Generalized additivity (two variables only): s = (∂f/∂x₁)/(∂f/∂x₂) must be multiplicatively separable (Eq. 3). Test (Appendix B): with F = ln s, S = |F_xy|² / (|F_xx F_yy| + |F_xy|²); accept if the median of S over the data is below 0.1 ("produced robust results"). Then g′ and 1/h′ are solved as one-dimensional problems and integrated symbolically; if that fails a net of the form F[g(x₁)+h(x₂)] is trained and its three parts are solved. "It is important to use smooth (not, e.g., ReLU) activation functions" — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Choosing among decompositions: "at each step we compare the decomposition in a unified way and try only the most accurate one", so run time grows roughly linearly with the number of inputs. For symmetry-type candidates the error is f(x̃) − f(x) at a point x̃ with the same h; for separability the fourth corner of a rectangle is predicted from the other three (Sec. 2.2) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Hypothesis-test rejection in brute force: data points are put in random order; a candidate is dropped as soon as z = √m (mean loss over first m points − record holder's loss)/σ exceeds ν = 10, which needs "no more than a handful of evaluations" per candidate (Eq. 4) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- Surrogate: 4 hidden layers (128, 128, 64, 64), tanh, 80/20 split, Adam, learning rate 10⁻² reduced to 10⁻⁵ on plateaus (Sec. 2.4). Brute force is allowed "only a minute for each iteration" (Sec. 4) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)

#### KAN (KAN1), symbolic-regression parts
- Model: layers of learnable one-dimensional functions on edges, each ϕ(x) = w_b·silu(x) + w_s·spline(x) with B-splines (usually cubic) on G intervals; nodes only sum (Sec. 2.2, Eq. 2.10 to 2.12) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- Sparsification: the L1 norm of an edge function is its mean absolute value over the inputs; loss = prediction loss + λ(μ₁ Σ|Φ|₁ + μ₂ Σ S(Φ)), where S is the entropy of the normalised edge norms in a layer; "we find L1 to be insufficient ... an additional entropy regularization is necessary" (Sec. 2.5.1, Eq. 2.17 to 2.20) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- Pruning: a node is kept if both its largest incoming and its largest outgoing edge norm exceed θ = 10⁻² (Eq. 2.21) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- Symbolification: for a chosen library function f, fit y ≈ c·f(a·x + b) + d to the edge's inputs and outputs "by iterative grid search of a, b and linear regression"; `suggest_symbolic` proposes candidates and `auto_symbolic` takes the top one for every edge; the affine parameters are then trained further, "and when we see the loss dropping to machine precision, we know that we have found the correct symbolic expression" (Sec. 2.5.1, 2.5.2, Table 6) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- The paper does not give the ranking rule. In the current pykan code, `suggest_symbolic` ranks by 0.8 × (hand-set complexity of the function, 0 to 5) + 0.2 × log₂(1 + 10⁻⁵ − R²) and returns the top 5; `auto_symbolic` has `r2_threshold=0.0` by default; the library has about 30 entries (powers x to x⁵, inverse powers, sqrt, exp, log, abs, sin, cos, tan, tanh, sgn, arcsin, arccos, arctan, arctanh, 0, gaussian) — [pykan MultKAN.py](https://github.com/KindXiaoming/pykan/blob/master/kan/MultKAN.py); [pykan utils.py](https://github.com/KindXiaoming/pykan/blob/master/kan/utils.py)
- KAN1 has no symmetry or separability test; structure is read off the pruned graph by a person or by `auto_symbolic` (Sec. 2.5.2, 4.1) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)

#### KAN 2.0 (KAN2)
- Adds multiplication nodes (MultKAN), so x·y needs one node and linear edges instead of the identity (x+y)² − (x−y)² (Sec. 2, Fig. 2) — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- Attribution score replaces the L1 norm for pruning: scores are propagated from the output back to the inputs using standard deviations of edge and node activations (Eq. 9), because "even if a node or an edge has a large norm itself, it may not contribute to the output" (Sec. 4.1) — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- Structure tests on any trained network through its derivatives (Sec. 4.2.2): additive separability if the Hessian block between the two groups is zero; multiplicative if the same holds for the Hessian of log|f| (Eq. 10, 11); "generalized separability" f = F(g + h) if the ratio of first derivatives across groups is multiplicatively separable (Eq. 12, 13); generalized symmetry if the unit gradient with respect to the group does not depend on the other inputs, ∇_z(∇̂_y f) = 0 (Eq. 14, 15). The three form a hierarchy (Eq. 16) and are applied recursively to turn a function into a tree — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- No thresholds are stated; the authors give two caveats: "(1) results can be seed and/or threshold-dependent. (2) all tests rely on second-order derivatives, which may not be robust due to the model being trained only on zero-order information" (Sec. 4.2.2) — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- Three "tricks" for symbolic regression (Sec. 4.3): A, train a large net, extract the tree, build a second net with that modular structure, then symbolify; B, sparse initialisation; C, hypothesis testing by branching from saved checkpoints and comparing losses at equal complexity (example: 6.5 × 10⁻⁴ against 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ test RMSE for two hypotheses with two edges each) — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)

#### KAN-SR
- Workflow (Sec. 2.1): 1 scale inputs; 2 optional brute-force fit of a small template library (for example θ₁x₀x₁ + θ₂, θ₁x₀/(x₁+θ₂) + θ₃, up to four-variable products), each fitted with BFGS and accepted if below a threshold; 3 single-layer KAN with one sum or product unit; 4 single-layer KAN with several units; 5 symmetry and separability tests, recursing on sub-problems; 6 output transformation (log, exp, ...) and rerun; 7 optional deeper KANs. The pipeline returns early when a match is found — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- Edge functions are sums of five radial basis functions w_i(s_i − tanh²((x − c_i)/h_i)), 20 parameters per edge; a unit is either a sum or a product of its edges over all inputs (Sec. 2.3) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- Tests are AIF1's, run on "a lightweight interpolating network": separability errors are L1 norms of y − (f_S + f_S̄ − f₀) and y − f_S·f_S̄/f₀ divided by the validation error, accepted if below 10; pair symmetry by the four transformations (x_i+a, x_j+a), (x_i+a, x_j−a), (a·x_i, x_j/a), (a·x_i, a·x_j) (Sec. 2.4) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- Ordering rule: "Symmetries are evaluated first because they reduce dimensionality without relying on model-generated targets. Separabilities depend on the network output and may introduce an additional approximation error"; when several pass, the one with the lowest relative error is applied (Sec. 2.4) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- Extraction: each edge is fitted against a library (polynomials, trigonometric, exponential, logarithmic, Michaelis-Menten); the choice is either lowest MSE or Score = MSE · 5^Θ with Θ a hand-set complexity per function; expressions are combined, simplified with SymPy and constants re-optimised with BFGS (Sec. 2.5) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)

#### Symbolic metamodels (SM) and the follow-up (SMPF)
- SM: approximate a trained black-box f by g(x) = Σ_i g_i^out(Σ_j g_ij^in(x_j)) (Kolmogorov form), each univariate function a Meijer G-function whose real parameters are trained by gradient descent on the squared error between g and f at points drawn uniformly from the unit cube; in practice r = 1, the outer function is the identity and products x_i x_j are added as extra inputs (Sec. 3.1, 3.2, Algorithm 1) — [NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/567b8f5f423af15818a068235807edc0-Paper.pdf)
- SM has no structure tests. If the trained G-function is not in the wanted class (polynomial, algebraic, closed form), the parameters are perturbed at random within a ball of radius δ to find one that is; failing that, a Chebyshev polynomial approximation is returned (Algorithm 1) — [NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/567b8f5f423af15818a068235807edc0-Paper.pdf)
- SMPF replaces G-functions by five parametrised primitives (cubic polynomial, a·e^(−bx), a·sin(bx+c), a·log(bx+c), a·x/(bx²+cx+d)) on the edges of a three-layer tree g(x) = Σ_i g_hi(Σ_j g_ij(x_j)); a genetic search changes the tree and the primitive on each edge, gradient descent trains the parameters, fitness is MSE + λ·(number of edges) (Sec. 2, 3.1, 3.2, Eq. 4, 7) — [2302.04791](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04791)

#### Cranmer et al. 2020
- Four steps: design a network with separable internal parts (a graph network with edge, node and global functions), train it end to end, fit a symbolic expression to each internal function separately, and substitute the expressions back, refitting constants on the data (Sec. 2) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)
- The decomposition comes from the architecture, not from a test: with z = f(Σ_j g(x_j)), fitting f and g separately needs 2 × 10⁹ candidate equations instead of 10¹⁸ (Sec. 2) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)
- Messages are made low-dimensional by a bottleneck, an L1 penalty (weight 10⁻²) or a KL penalty; the message components with the largest standard deviation are the ones fitted (Sec. 4.1, Appendix C) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)
- Symbolic step: Eureqa (PySR for the controlled table) on 5000 input-output samples of the learned function, minimising mean absolute error, with operators +, −, ×, /, >, <, ^, exp, log, IF and real constants (Appendix C) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)

#### Liu and Tegmark, hidden symmetries (brief)
- A "generalized symmetry" is a linear differential operator L with L·T = 0 (translation invariance, Lie invariance and equivariance, Hamiltonicity, modularity; Table 1). An invertible network z′ = z + f_NN(z) (two hidden layers of 400, SiLU) is trained to minimise ⟨|L T|²⟩/⟨|z|²⟩^α in the new coordinates; a symmetry is "tentatively discovered" if the loss falls below 10⁻³, after which AIF is run on the learned transformation (Sec. II, III.1) — [2109.09721](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.09721)
- The tensor field T is known in closed form in all examples; the unknown is the coordinate change, not the function (Sec. II.2) — [2109.09721](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.09721)

#### Taylor genetic programming (TaylorGP)
- No surrogate network: a k-th order Taylor polynomial around one data point is obtained by solving the exactly determined linear system F ≈ D A⁻¹ built from the nearest points (Eq. 2, 3). An n-variable order-k polynomial has C(n+k, n) terms, so "we usually set k = 1 or 2 in a high-dimensional dataset" (Sec. 3.1) — [2205.09751](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.09751)
- Features read from the coefficients (Sec. 3.2): low-order polynomial if all coefficients above some degree are zero; additive separability if every mixed-variable coefficient is zero; multiplicative separability by the same test on log y; bounds by interval arithmetic on the polynomial; monotonicity from the data; parity if only odd or only even powers remain — [2205.09751](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.09751)
- Thresholding: coefficients below a threshold are set to zero (10⁻⁴ in the worked example, Sec. 6.2.1); "it is not easy to get a suitable threshold for the Taylor polynomial because of the diversity of datasets" (Sec. 6.2.3) — [2205.09751](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.09751)
- Use: split by separability, return a polynomial directly if detected, otherwise run genetic programming whose initial individuals and recombination respect bounds, monotonicity and parity through composition rules (Table 1, Algorithm 1) — [2205.09751](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.09751)

#### NestyNet-SR (not on the list; preprint v2 of 25 August 2026)
- Stage A fits a "NestyNet" surrogate with closed-form derivatives and tests for additive, multiplicative and compound-coordinate structure, recursing until each branch is a low-arity "neural atom"; Stage B turns atoms into closed form by rewrite rules and, if needed, a factorised search (Sec. III, Fig. 1) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- Stated reason for a new surrogate: separability tests need accurate mixed second derivatives, and "when Hessian elements carry ∼10% relative error, testing whether ∂²f/∂x_i∂x_j ≈ 0 becomes unreliable" (Sec. II.2) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- Tests (Sec. IV.2): additive, mixed Hessian block ≈ 0; multiplicative, f·f_ij − f_i·f_j ≈ 0, evaluated without taking a logarithm and masking small |f|; offset-multiplicative f = c + g·h; variable non-dependence from gradient magnitude; inputs not under test are frozen at their medians. Tolerance for the mixed-derivative test is 10⁻³ (Table 3) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- Monomial coordinate (Sec. IV.3): if f = g(Π x_i^(a_i)) then the weighted gradients x_i ∂f/∂x_i are collinear with a; the N × n matrix of weighted gradients is tested for rank 1 by SVD (accept if σ₂/σ₁ ≤ 0.1, Table 3), the right singular vector gives a, which is snapped to integers and checked by fit quality. A log-derivative variant (x_i/f)∂f/∂x_i is used when f spans many decades — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- One linear test for all point symmetries (Sec. VI.1): require ∇f(x)·(Ax + b) − α − βf(x) = 0 at every sample. This is linear in the n² + n + 2 unknowns (A, b, α, β), so each sample is one row and the symmetries are the null space, found by one SVD. An accepted generator gives the form f = −α/β + e^(βs)H(z) (β ≠ 0) or f = αs + H(z) (β = 0), with z the invariant coordinates and s the coordinate along the symmetry (Eq. 7 to 9) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- Same test in other charts (Sec. VI.2): in u_i = log x_i scalings become translations, so monomials appear as linear invariants whose components "are snapped to small rationals and revalidated against the determining residual"; u_i = 1/x_i exposes Σ c_i/x_i. A hidden additive form f = g(Σ c_i φ_i(x_i)) is certified by the ratio R_ij = f_ij/(f_i f_j) being the same for every pair — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- Safeguards: work with the null-space subspace, not single basis vectors; require closure of the Lie bracket, stable rank, held-out residuals and bootstrap principal angles; a reduction is promoted only if it survives "a complexity-penalized comparison against the unreduced expression at matched held-out accuracy" (Sec. VI.1) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- With trained surrogates, "whose gradients carry relative errors of order 10⁻³, the global nullspace solve can lose bracket closure", so coordinates are also built from pairwise one-dimensional scaling, translation, rotation and boost tests, chained together (Sec. VI.2) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)

### Inferences
- The AIF1 translation test evaluates the surrogate at x + 1 for inputs sampled in [1, 5], so part of the test points lie outside the training range; the paper does not discuss this, while AIF2 says "we avoid using our neural network ... outside the domain where it was trained" ([2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782), Sec. 3).
- In AIF2 Appendix A the text says V is "one minus the smallest eigenvalue" but the formula maximises over μ, which gives the largest eigenvalue; I read it as the largest.
- The tests fall into three accuracy classes: values only (AIF1, KAN-SR), first derivatives (AIF2 symmetry and compositionality, NestyNet-SR monomial and determining-equation tests), second or higher derivatives (KAN2 separability, NestyNet-SR separability, AIF2 generalized additivity, which differentiates a log of a gradient ratio twice). The authors' own caveats get stronger in that order.
- The determining equation of NestyNet-SR is the same kind of object as our rules that look for linear relations between a hole's values and derivatives at the data.

### Gaps
- The order of modules in AIF1 is only in its Fig. 1, which I could not read; the order above is that of Sec. II.
- AIF2 says the supplement "details how our test points are chosen"; Appendices A to C do not give this for simple symmetry and separability.
- KAN2 gives no numerical thresholds for its tests; I did not read the pykan test functions.
- KAN-SR does not state the symmetry threshold, the shift constant a, the network used for the tests, or any hyperparameter values.

## 2. Data regime, compute budget, and how results change with fewer points or more noise

### Takeaway
The surrogate step is the data-hungry part: AIF1 needed 10² to 10⁶ points whenever a neural test was used, and the newest method still uses 2000 + 2000 points. Noise at the 1% level already removes a large share of exact recoveries in every paper that reports it; nobody reports a surrogate-based result at about 100 noisy points.

### Cited Findings
- AIF1 data: 10⁵ rows per equation, inputs uniform in [1, 5] (ranges adjusted for some), 1 to 9 inputs; limit 2 hours of CPU per equation (Sec. III.1, III.2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- AIF1, fewer points: the data set was shrunk by factors of 10 until failure. "Most equations are discovered by the polynomial fit and brute force methods using only 10 data points"; equations that need the network "need significantly more data points (between 10² and 10⁶)" (Sec. III.3) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- My tally of the "Data needed" column of AIF1 Tables 4 and 5: 82 equations at 10 points, 13 at 10², 3 at 10³, 1 at 10⁴, 1 at 10⁶. ## 4. How the final formula is chosen and how constants are snapped

### Takeaway
Three selection schemes appear: a threshold plus shortest description (AIF1), a Pareto frontier of description length against error with held-out ranking (AIF2, and in a stricter statistical form NestyNet-SR), and the steepest drop in log error per unit of complexity (Cranmer). Snapping is spelled out only in the AIF line: nearest integer, then continued-fraction rationals, each accepted only if total description length falls.

### Cited Findings
- AIF1: among brute-force candidates with r.m.s. error below a threshold, pick the smallest DL = log₂N + λ·log₂[max(1, ε/ε_d)], N the rank of the string in the enumeration, ε_d = 10⁻¹⁵, λ = N_d^(1/2) "to prioritize simpler formulas" (Eq. 2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- AIF1 has no fitted real constants: numbers are built from the symbols 0, 1, increment, decrement and π, plus one additive or multiplicative constant; the authors name numerically optimised parameter symbols as a missing feature (Sec. II.4, III.5) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- Cranmer on this point: AIF "is unable to fit the constants required for the tasks here" (Sec. 2) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)
- AIF2 complexity (Table 2): natural number n costs log₂n bits; integer m, log₂(1+|m|); rational m/n, log₂[(1+|m|)n]; real r, ½log₂(1 + (r/ε)²) with ε = 2⁻³⁰; a formula with n distinct basis functions used k times costs k·log₂n plus its parameters. Example: m×v×v/2 costs log₂3 + 6·log₂4 ≈ 13.6 bits — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- AIF2 accuracy: mean error description length, the mean of L_d(error_i) with the real-number formula above, instead of mean squared or maximum error, for robustness to outliers (Sec. 2.2, Fig. 4) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- AIF2 frontier handling: each module returns a frontier; when two modules are combined the n₁·n₂ products are pruned to the non-dominated ones at once (Sec. 2.2) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- AIF2 snapping: after the recursion, add versions where rationals are replaced by reals fitted by gradient descent, then versions with zero, integer and rational snap "applied to all real-valued parameters as described in [27]", pruning after each step; "if there are 3 real-valued parameters, integer-snap generates 3 new models where the 1, 2 and 3 parameters closest to integers get rounded" (Sec. 2.2) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- The cited rule: replace a parameter by its nearest integer "if this reduces the total description length", starting with the parameter closest to an integer and "refitting for the other parameters after each successful 'integer snap'"; for rationals, "perform a continued fraction expansion of p and use each series truncation as a rational candidate", try first the candidate minimising (bits of the rational) + (bits of the error |p − q|), accept only snaps that reduce total description length (Sec. II.4) — [1810.10525](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.10525)
- Named failure modes of snapping: "undersnapping" and "oversnapping" (Sec. III) — [1810.10525](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.10525)
- AIF2 final pick for scoring: the frontier is built on 90% of the data and ranked by error description length on the other 10%; success only if the top-ranked formula is exact, or irrational parameters are within 0.01% (Appendix C) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- AIF2 on its complexity measure: it "is dependent on the chosen set of operations and does not always match our intuition"; in its Fig. 1 an unfamiliar mc²(1/cos(v/c) − 1) sits on the frontier between mv²/2 and the exact formula (Appendix C) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- KAN1 and KAN2: edges are replaced one at a time by c·f(ax+b)+d; a, b, c, d stay real; rounding is left to the user ("Perhaps she wants to cross out some small terms and snap coefficient to small integers", KAN1 Sec. 4.4). A set of formulas of growing size is obtained by training KANs of different shapes (KAN1 Table 4) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756); [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- KAN-SR: per edge, lowest MSE or MSE·5^Θ; then constants are re-optimised with BFGS; the pipeline stops at the first candidate under an error threshold, else returns the best found. The authors mention AIC, BIC and multi-objective selection as possible but unused (Sec. 2.1, 2.5) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- Cranmer: complexity counts operators, constants and variables, with ^, exp, log, IF weighted three times; from the best formula at each complexity take the one maximising −Δlog(MAE)/Δc; constants are refit on the data after substitution (Sec. 4.1, Appendix C, Table 4, Fig. 6) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)
- SM and SMPF: no selection among formulas beyond the loss (SMPF adds λ × number of edges); constants stay real (see the SMPF expression in question 3) — [NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/567b8f5f423af15818a068235807edc0-Paper.pdf); [2302.04791](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04791)
- SRSD on selection: PySR was the only baseline none of whose solutions with a dummy variable reached R² > 0.999, which "suggest[s] that it may be important in SRSD problems to penalize overcomplex solutions in a similar way to PySR" (Sec. 5.3) — [2206.10540](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.10540)
- NestyNet-SR default acceptance: a rewrite is accepted when the loss improves beyond a tolerance; separability rewrites get an extra loss allowance "of order ten percent", "since structure discovery is worth a small accuracy cost"; once both losses are below the noise floor the comparison switches to simplicity (Sec. VII.4) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- NestyNet-SR statistical selection: an audit sample is sealed before the search; afterwards candidate i removes j only if i is no more complex in every component (size, depth, fitted coefficients, constant description length) and a simultaneous one-sided 95% bootstrap bound on the paired loss difference is negative; the reported law is found by starting from the simplest candidate and accepting more complexity "only when the lower confidence bound on improvement ... exceeds δ" (δ = 0) (Sec. VII.5) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)
- NestyNet-SR snapping: exponents from the SVD are snapped to integers and checked by fit; half-integer powers are recovered later by templates; symmetry covectors are snapped to small rationals and rechecked (Sec. IV.3, VI.2) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)

### Inferences
- With N = 100 points the BIC charge per real parameter is ½·ln(100) ≈ 2.3 nats ≈ 3.3 bits. AIF2's table charges 1.6 bits for the integer 2, 2 bits for 1/2 and 3 bits for 3/2, against about 30 bits for a generic real at ε = 2⁻³⁰. So in our score a snapped small rational would save at most 1 to 2 bits against BIC: snapping matters for exact-recovery scoring and readability more than for the ranking at this N.
- NestyNet-SR's statistical selection lowers its recovery at every noise level relative to the heuristic (103 against 105, 91 against 94, 54 against 56), by the authors' own account because it declines candidates it cannot defend; this is the measured price of a conservative rule.

### Gaps
- AIF2 does not say how the single formula is picked from the frontier for a user who has no held-out truth; "convex corners" are mentioned only in the caption of its Fig. 1.
- KAN-SR's Θ values and error threshold are not given.

## 5. Concrete techniques we could adopt, and what each would buy

### Takeaway
The most transferable items are tests that are linear in the surrogate, because a GP gives a posterior for any linear functional: the affine determining equation and its log chart (NestyNet-SR), the rank-one test on weighted gradients for a whole monomial at once, AIF2's gradient-direction test to pick which inputs to merge before knowing the formula, and AIF1's value-only separability test. Next come the AIF snapping rule and description-length costs, a cheap template pass before the search, and using one-dimensional library fits only to shortlist.

### Cited Findings
- Determining equation ∇f·(Ax+b) − α − βf = 0, linear in (A, b, α, β); null space by one SVD; output forms f = −α/β + e^(βs)H(z) or f = αs + H(z); log and reciprocal charts; rational snapping of covectors; pairwise fallback at gradient error about 10⁻³ (Sec. ### Inferences
Each item below is my mapping onto the prototype; none is tested.

1. Determining equation in log coordinates, on the hole's posterior gradient. What: stack rows [∂H/∂log x ⊗ (log x, 1), −1, −H] at the data and take the null space; snap the invariant covector to small rationals. Buys: one linear solve proposes a full merged input Π x_i^(a_i) together with the outer form (a factor e^(βs), i.e. a power-law factor a(x), or an additive term αs, i.e. w·b(x)), which is exactly our term shape a(x)·H(u(x)) + w·b(x). Our existing rules are presumably special cases; the new parts would be the log and reciprocal charts, working with the whole null space when it has more than one dimension, and the pairwise fallback. Caveat: NestyNet-SR reports that the global solve already degrades at gradient errors of 10⁻³, and a GP on 100 noisy points will be far worse, so the rows should be weighted by the GP's posterior gradient covariance and the proposal accepted by evidence, never by the residual.
2. Rank-one test on weighted gradients. Buys: replaces a chain of pairwise merges (x₁/x₂, then ·x₃, ...) by one step, which shortens the beam path for power-law-heavy Feynman formulas and avoids intermediate states that score badly. Cheap (an n × n eigenproblem).
3. AIF2's subset test V to order merge candidates. Buys: tells which pair or triple enters through one unknown scalar before any formula for it is tried, so the beam can try merges only on the best subset. Uses gradient directions only, so it is unaffected by an outer function. Needs gradients at mixed points (x′ from one data point, x″ from another), which a GP provides with uncertainty.
4. Gradient-alignment score for a proposed u(x). Buys: a pre-screen costing O(n) per candidate before the O(n³) evidence, useful if the list of merged-input candidates grows.
5. Value-only separability as a pre-filter for additive and multiplicative splits. The quantity f(x) − f(x₁,c₂) − f(c₁,x₂) + f(c) is linear in the GP, so it has an exact posterior mean and variance; a z-score replaces AIF1's "10 × validation error". Buys: ordering of candidate partitions for 4 or 5 inputs (all subsets are 2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1) without any derivative. The evidence of the additive model remains the decision.
6. Snapping pass on the finalists. Integer snap closest-first with refit, then continued-fraction candidates, accept when (evidence − cost) does not fall, with AIF2's bit costs for the snapped constant in place of the BIC charge. Buys: exact exponents and constants (needed for "solution rate" metrics on Feynman and SRSD) at the cost of a handful of extra evidence evaluations. See the inference in question 4 on the small size of the score gain at N = 100.
7. Symbol costs from AIF2's k·log₂n in place of fixed per-symbol costs. Buys: a cost that grows with the size of the vocabulary actually used, and a common scale (bits) with the constants. Low effort; effect on rankings unknown.
8. Template and polynomial pass before the GP search. A log-linear fit for a pure monomial and a degree ≤ 4 polynomial fit take milliseconds; KAN-SR's Easy-set jump from 60% (PySR) to 93% is consistent with many Easy SRSD problems being of this type, though the paper does not break this down. Buys: time for the hard cases within the 10 to 60 s budget.
9. Library-function rule: use the KAN-style fit c·f(a·u+b)+d to the hole's posterior mean only to shortlist the top few functions, then decide by evidence. The in-context paper is direct evidence that deciding on the one-dimensional fit alone is fragile.
10. Output transforms with the right bookkeeping. AIF2's positivity rule and NestyNet-SR's asinh retry suggest proposing the outer function early when y is positive or spans decades. For us the evidence under a transformed y needs the Jacobian term to be comparable with the untransformed model.
11. Units, if the benchmark protocol allows them: AIF1's null-space reduction lowers the input dimension before the GP sees the data, which at 100 points is worth more than any later test. AIF1 (100 against 93) and NestyNet-SR both lean on units.
12. Bounds and monotonicity of a one-dimensional hole as filters on the library (a monotone hole rules out sin over more than half a period, a bounded one rules out exp and powers). TaylorGP's Table 4 says these two features are the reliable ones.
13. Conservative choice among near-tied finalists: start from the simplest and accept a more complex one only if a paired comparison (for example leave-one-out predictive log density differences with their standard error) clears a bound, as in NestyNet-SR Sec. VII.5. Buys: protection against over-complex answers at 100 points; costs some recoveries (see question 4).
14. Not worth adopting at our scale: AIF2's sequential z-test (the evidence is not a per-point sum and n is 100); Meijer G-functions (1 to 4 s per evaluation by SMPF's account); "set two variables equal" (needs the surrogate on the diagonal, usually outside the data when inputs have different ranges); TaylorGP's coefficient thresholding for separability (12.5% correct).

### Gaps
- Whether GP posterior gradients from about 100 noisy points are accurate enough for any of items 1 to 5 is not answered by these papers; the only number is NestyNet-SR's 10⁻³ gradient accuracy for certified symmetry discovery, obtained with a different surrogate and 2000 clean points.
- No paper compares value-based and derivative-based separability tests on the same surrogate.

## 6. Overlap with GP holes: what is the same, what differs

### Takeaway
The rewrite vocabulary is shared (pair merges, monomial coordinates, additive and multiplicative splits, one outer function, a library of one-dimensional functions), and NestyNet-SR's syntax tree with "neural atoms" next to symbolic nodes is close to our partial expressions. None of the papers keeps an unresolved nonparametric part in the reported answer as a scored outcome, none scores partial expressions by an exact likelihood, and none shows a surrogate-based result at about 100 points.

### Cited Findings
- AIF1 and AIF2 generate the sub-problem data from the surrogate (y′ = f_NN(x₁, c₂)), and an unsolved sub-problem means no formula for the whole (AIF1 Sec. II.5.3; AIF2 Sec. 4 on the unsolved α-term) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481); [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- AIF2 decides between decompositions by test accuracy and between formulas by description length; AIF1 by thresholds relative to network validation error (AIF2 Sec. 2.2; AIF1 Table 2) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782); [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- KAN1 on non-symbolic parts: "when the target function is not symbolic, symbolic regression will fail but KANs can still provide something meaningful. For example, a special function (e.g., a Bessel function) ... KANs can use splines to approximate it numerically anyway (see Figure 4.1 (d))" (Sec. 2.5.2) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- KAN2 has edges where "both symbolic and spline parts are active" during interactive refinement (Sec. 5.4), and compares hypotheses by test loss at equal number of edges (Sec. 4.3) — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- KAN-SR: "If the threshold is not reached after a full completion of the algorithm, the best equation found during the search is returned" (Fig. 1 caption); its noise model is stated as y = Θ*(x, θ*) + Gaussian noise and BIC is named as a typical objective, but the method uses MSE thresholds (Sec. 2, 2.5) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- SM can end with a non-closed-form part: SMPF notes that "for almost all parameters, Meijer G-functions do not have familiar closed form representation" and shows a symbolic-pursuit result that stays a G-function (Sec. 1, 5.1) — [2302.04791](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04791)
- Cranmer replaces network parts one function at a time and could stop part-way, but reports only fully symbolic replacements (Sec. 2) — [2006.11287](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11287)
- NestyNet-SR: every expression is a tree whose leaf atom "is a neural atom while it wraps a NestyNet surrogate (its state throughout Stage A and in the unresolved branches of Stage B) and an analytical leaf once it has been rewritten in closed form"; trees are fitted jointly by Levenberg-Marquardt and compared by loss with tolerances; "The final output is a human-readable analytical expression" (Sec. - NestyNet-SR's output forms from a symmetry, f = −α/β + e^(βs)H(z) and f = αs + H(z), have an unknown function H of invariant coordinates times or plus a known part (Eq. 9) — [2608.21051](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21051)

### Inferences
| | Same as GP holes | Different |
|---|---|---|
| AIF1 / AIF2 | Pair merges by +, −, ×, /; additive and multiplicative splits; one outer function via transforms of y; composition g(h(x)); snapping; complexity-penalised choice | Surrogate is a throw-away network; sub-problems are data generated from it; decisions by thresholds (AIF1) or description length (AIF2); answer must be fully symbolic; 10⁵ points; hours |
| KAN1 / KAN2 | A model that is part symbolic, part flexible one-dimensional functions; library replacement of one function at a time; KAN2's derivative tests match our derivative rules in kind | Flexible parts are only one-dimensional, so multi-input structure must come from the network shape; no probabilistic score, loss plus sparsity penalties; choice by a person or by per-edge R²; no multi-input hole can remain |
| KAN-SR | Recursive simplification then one-dimensional library; noise model written as Gaussian | Tests are AIF1's on a separate network; single-layer sum or product form only; MSE thresholds; first match wins |
| SM / SMPF | Sum of terms with univariate building blocks | Target is a trained black box queried at will; no structure tests; no noise model |
| Cranmer | Decompose, then fit symbols to parts; refit on the data | Decomposition is built into the architecture by the user; large data |
| LT | Symmetry as a linear differential condition | The function is known; the search is over coordinate changes |
| TaylorGP | Reads separability, low-order polynomial and parity from a local model | Local Taylor polynomial at one point; thresholded coefficients; genetic search does the rest |
| NestyNet-SR | Partial expressions with unresolved nonparametric atoms fitted jointly with symbolic parts; symmetry from a linear system in values and gradients; forms a(x)·H(z) and αs + H(z); monomial coordinates; rational snapping; tiered rules | Atoms are deterministic surrogates fitted by least squares; acceptance by loss tolerances or a held-out audit; atoms are meant to be eliminated; 2000 + 2000 clean points, units given, minutes to an hour per problem |

- Unresolved nonparametric part kept in the answer: no method reports it as an accepted, scored outcome. Closest are KAN (a spline edge may stay, shown for one Bessel example) and NestyNet-SR (neural atoms are the intermediate state and would be what is left if Stage B fails, but the paper reports only fully closed forms).
- Exact likelihood for partial expressions: none. The nearest are description length on full formulas (AIF2) and a held-out statistical audit on full formulas (NestyNet-SR).
- About 100 points: only the non-surrogate parts of AIF1 (polynomial fit and brute force, 10 to 100 clean points). No surrogate-based test is shown below 10² points, and none with noise at that size.
- One structural difference follows from the first two: because our score is computed on the real data for the partial expression itself, a wrong reading of the surrogate costs a wasted candidate, whereas in AIF-type recursion it corrupts the data of every later sub-problem (KAN-SR's ordering rule exists to limit this).

### Gaps
- I did not search for GP-surrogate or Bayesian variants of the decomposition idea outside the listed papers; another researcher's scope may cover them.

## 7. Limitations stated by the authors

### Takeaway
The recurring admissions are: the surrogate's error acts as noise and breaks tests (AIF1, KAN2, NestyNet-SR, TaylorGP); tests based on second derivatives are fragile; one-dimensional leftovers that are not in the library defeat the method; thresholds and complexity measures are hand-set; and cost grows fast with the number of inputs.

### Cited Findings
- AIF1: the 100 Feynman equations "should be viewed as a training set for our AI Feynman algorithm, since we made improvements to its implementation and hyper-parameters to optimize performance" (Sec. III.5) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- AIF1: wants network error near 10⁻⁶; symmetry search covers only +, −, ×, / of two variables; no numerically optimised constants; a genetic method "perform[s] quite well even in presense of significant noise" and returns a frontier, which AIF1 does not (Sec. IV.2) — [1905.11481](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.11481)
- AIF2: the modularity methods "only help for functions of more than one variable"; results depend on the function set and brute-force time; complexity "does not always match our intuition"; "Larger and more challenging collections of science-based equations are needed" (Sec. 4, Appendix C) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- AIF2 on overfitting: "Overfitting noise would generally reduce apparent graph modularity, thus causing failure to discover formulas rather than discovery of spurious 'overfit' formulas" (Sec. 3) — [2006.10782](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.10782)
- KAN1: results "can depend on random seeds. With some unlucky seed, the pruned network could be larger than needed"; too small a grid makes the network "use the ensembling strategy, making interpretation harder" (Appendix C); "the biggest bottleneck of KANs lies in its slow training"; on fully automatic use, "Although it is not likely for KANs to return the correct answer immediately, KANs will always return something useful" (Sec. 6, 4.4) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- KAN1 on needing extra assumptions: "KANs need additional inductive biases to further simplify these equations" to reach the known knot-theory formula (Sec. 4.3) — [2404.19756](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19756)
- KAN2: test results are seed- and threshold-dependent and rest on second derivatives; "a KAN may only remain interpretable when the network scale is relatively small"; the paper focuses "primarily on small-scale, physics-related examples"; in one case "prior knowledge appears harmful" (Sec. 4.2.2, 5.4, 7) — [2408.10205](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10205)
- KAN-SR: "there is no universal KAN architecture that can accommodate all target functions, even when strong regularization is applied" (Sec. 2.1); "The observed superior performance is only obtained when single-layer representations combined with the simplifications and the univariate library are enough"; "if the optimal solution lies outside this constrained space, KAN-SR often fails to produce a good fitting equation, unlike other methods"; larger KANs become "diffuse" and add hyperparameters; all benchmark data came from closed-form equations with all variables present (Sec. 4) — [2509.10089](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10089)
- SM: "appropriate for models with small to moderate number of features" (Sec. 1) — [NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/567b8f5f423af15818a068235807edc0-Paper.pdf)
- SMPF on SM and on itself: SM "cannot capture interaction of more than two features" and needs C(d,2) + d G-functions; SMPF "lacks theoretical guarantees", has "several hyperparameters ... to specify structure of the tree", "cannot handle high dimension inputs", and "the richness of functions we can create is limited" (Sec. 4, 6) — [2302.04791](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.04791)

- LT: "our method can only search for symmetries from a list of candidates pre-specified by the user, and cannot search for unknown symmetries"; the examples involve no more than two symmetries at once (Sec. IV) — [2109.09721](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.09721)
- TaylorGP: "TaylorGP will degrade when the dataset dimension increases because of the local approximation of the Taylor polynomial"; the features "may be incorrect or incomplete" (Sec. 6.2.2, 7) — [2205.09751](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.09751)
- NestyNet-SR: "The Stage B rewrite rules are heuristic in nature and may miss optimal analytical forms"; "deeply nested high-arity expressions remain challenging"; "Computational cost also scales exponentially with the number of variables for exhaustive separability search"; the statistical result "is conditional on the frozen archive and searched grammar"; the Feynman set "contains virtually no parameters inside nonlinear operators" (Sec. A.6.2) — [2206.10540](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.10540); [2107.14351](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.14351)

### Inferences
- The SRBench remark on structural assumptions applies to us: a sum of terms a(x)·H(u(x)) inside at most one outer function is such an assumption, and formulas outside it (for example a ratio of two sums) can only be reached with a hole left in place. Keeping the hole is what the other methods lack, but it will not count as a "solution" under SRBench or SRSD criteria.
- AIF2's claim that overfitting leads to failure and not to spurious formulas is argued, not measured; KAN2's x^(1/2) against x^(−1/2) example is a small counter-case where surrogate error picked a wrong simple form.

### Gaps
- None of the papers quantifies false-positive rates of its structure tests (how often a symmetry or split is accepted when absent); TaylorGP's Table 4 is the only per-feature accuracy table, and it counts only ground-truth positives.
- The NeurIPS 2019 supplement, which may contain further stated limitations of SM, was not read.
